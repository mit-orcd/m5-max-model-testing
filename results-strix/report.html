How many times does each test run? Coding suites (C, Python, Bash — easy, hard and brutal) and the research paper: 3 trials per task — trial 0 at temperature 0, trials 1–2 at 0.7, so a 1/3 pass is sampling luck, not reliability. Framing: 20 trials per wording. Decode speed: median of 3 repeated 2048-token generations — the tok/s column shows ±1 std across those runs (hover a cell for the exact count), as does the dot plot on the analysis page. Quality: 6 probes × 3 trials (temp 0, then 0.7×2). Concurrency: one pass per stream level (no repeats). Self-repair: one initial attempt, then up to 5 rounds of compiler feedback. Two writing tasks that are easy to confuse: tok/s is measured on a 1500-word essay about MIT (speed only, content not graded — each model's essay is on its detail page), while the research column is a different test: extracting NFS facts from RHEL 10 documentation. Dense vs mixture-of-experts

A dense model runs every parameter on every token; a mixture-of-experts model routes each token to a few of its experts, so only the active column does compute. That is why the 125B flash-next decodes like a 6B model and the whole top of the table is MoE — on memory-bandwidth-bound Apple silicon, active parameters are what you pay per token, and MoE buys a large total capacity at a small active cost. The last column, tok/s per active billion, is the efficiency of that trade; dense models are 1x by definition and omitted.

What it costs to get working code

Correctness alone picks a model that is right and unusably slow; tok/s alone picks one that is fast and wrong. The number that decides it is the two divided: seconds of wall clock per solution that passes.
